Probability, Distribution Topology & 2d20 Calibration in Satya

Dice are a tool to inject uncertainty, risk, and surprise into a roleplaying game. Whenever a character attempts an action where the outcome is uncertain and meaningful, the game's resolution engine must answer two questions: 1. Did the character succeed or fail? 2. By how much? (Did they barely scrape by, achieve a masterwork triumph, fail cleanly, or suffer a catastrophic botch?)

Most tabletop RPGs answer these questions using one of two classic probability distributions:

  • Flat Uniform Rolls (1d20 + Modifier vs. Target Number): Every number on the die is equally likely. Modifiers simply shift the win/loss cutoff linearly, producing "swingy" outcomes where mastery only shifts a flat threshold.
  • Additive Bell Curves (3d6 or NdK Summed Pools): By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), summing independent dice rapidly converges to a symmetric Gaussian (normal) bell curve. Modifiers only shift the mean (μ) or scale the variance (σ2); the fundamental shape of the curve never changes.

In Satya—a Computer-Assisted RPG (CARPG)—we want the shape (topology) of the probability distribution itself (its skewness, modality, and tail behavior) to reflect the narrative reality of the task. A master performing a routine task, a novice attempting a desperate gamble, and an exhausted veteran fighting through injury should not just sit at different points on the same bell curve—they should experience fundamentally different probability distributions.

This chapter details the mathematical foundation and calibration rationale behind Satya's Two-List Cascading ("Watershed") 2d20 Mechanic (for definitions of all mathematical variables and subscripts such as TNP, TNGM, CP, δover, and δunder, see the Mathematical Notation, Variables & Formula Glossary; for the step-by-step gameplay procedure, task gates, and worked examples, see Core 2d20 Cascade & Task Resolution).

1. Mathematical Structure of the Two-List Cascading ("Watershed") Process

Instead of rolling a single die or summing an independent pool, a task roll in Satya consists of N sequential pairs of 20-sided dice (K = 20), where N ∈ [1, 8] is the character's Skill Level:

List A = (A1, A2, …, AN),    List B = (B1, B2, …, BN)

At each step n ∈ {1, …, N}, the unmodified pair sum Xn = An + Bn ranges from 2 to 40, forming a discrete triangular probability mass function (PMF) centered at its exact 50% mean of 21.

1.1 Midpoint-Anchored Roll-Over Targets (TNP and Hidden TNGM)

Because each step sums two d20 dice (K = 20, mean 21) while a character's Controlling Characteristic CP (Control of the Player) is measured on a 1–20 scale (with the human population average at CP = 10), the base target is anchored at 1.5K + 1 = 31 so that an average character (CP = 10) using standard equipment (Δtool = 0, Known Tool Modifier) needs the exact 2d20 midpoint of 21:

TNP = (1.5K + 1) − CP + Δtool = 31 − CP + Δtool

where:

  • TNP (Target Number of the Player): The known roll-over threshold the player's pair total Yn must beat at each step n.
  • CP (Controlling Characteristic of the Player): The player's governing attribute (1–20 scale; higher CP lowers TNP).
  • Δtool (Known Tool & Loadout Modifier): Equipment bonus or penalty known to the player before rolling (-4 smart-rig to +8 improvised).

To preserve epistemic fog of war (see Section 6), Hidden External Task Difficulty (Δext) does not shift the player's target number TNP. Instead, the player always rolls their sequential cascade against their known personal target TNP, while the Game Master secretly rolls an opposing GM Resistance Roll (XGM = AGM + BGM) against the Target Number of the Game Master (TNGM):

TNGM = 21 − Δext    (equivalent to an opposing GM Controller CGM = 10 + Δext)

*(When evaluating uncontested baseline curves or known-difficulty benchmarks in Sections 4–5, TNeff = 31 − C + Δext denotes the Effective Uncontested Target Number on a single-side roll).*

Satya supports two primary state-transition modes in paired_list_signed_margin_pmfs and crates/satya-engine/src/dice_probabilities.rs:

  • Single-Step Markov Carry (markov_step / CarryMode::MarkovStep — Standard Resolution): Pair n receives a single +δover (+6) if Pair n−1 succeeded, or a single −δunder (−(6 + Δint)) if Pair n−1 failed. Mathematically, this forms a first-order Markov chain over {Success, Failure} states. It allows a skilled character who misses Pair 1 to fight through the −δunder penalty on Pair 2 or 3, recover their footing, and flip the cascade back into positive +δover momentum.
  • Cumulative Snowball Carry (cumulative_snowball / CarryMode::CumulativeSnowball — High-Stakes Resolution): Carry-over compounds across every step (Carryn+1 = Carryn ± δ, yielding +6, +12, +18... on streaks of success, or -6, -12, -18... on streaks of failure). This splits the probability distribution into a stark bimodal ("watershed") distribution where early momentum snowballs into effortless mastery and early failure locks out recovery.

2. Eliminating the "Skilled Botch Paradox"

In many dice-pool RPGs (such as classic World of Darkness or Shadowrun variants), rolling more dice for higher skill accidentally increases the probability of rolling 1s—penalizing masters for rolling more dice. Satya eliminates this paradox through two complementary mathematical properties of BotchRule:

  1. Monotonic Worst-Case Recovery (closest_miss / BotchRule::ClosestMiss — Default):
    • A botch failure only occurs when total_successes == 0.
    • Severity is determined by the character's best attempt across all N pairs, Ybest = max(Y1, …, YN), binned into 4-point (K/5 = 4) tiers below TNeff:
    • If TNeff − 4 ≤ Ybest < TNeff, the result is 0 (Clean Failure / Near Miss).
    • Every additional 4-point tier below TNeff adds -1 botch severity (-1 to -5).
    • Proof of Monotonicity: Because max(Y1, …, YN, YN+1) ≥ max(Y1, …, YN) for every realization of YN+1, adding another skill pair N → N+1 strictly decreases both overall failure probability and botch severity across every tier.
  2. Impossible Floor Breach (ceiling_breach & SuccessWithComplication):
    • Because two d20 dice have a physical minimum sum of 2, an adjusted pair total Yn = An + Bn − δunder can only drop below 2 (Yn < 2) if the character failed the previous pair (incurring −δunder) and rolled near snake-eyes on the current pair (raw_botches += 1).
    • When total_successes >= 1 and raw_botches >= 1, the cascade produces SuccessWithComplication (OutcomeTier::PartialSuccess)—modeling a skilled operator who recovers from an early stumble to complete the task, but suffers strain, noise, or equipment wear from the floor-breach step.

Rather than inventing arbitrary target numbers for different tasks (e.g., "Picking this lock is TN 15, shooting at 100m is TN 28"), Satya uses a single universal baseline target (TN0 = 21 at average C = 10) and separates every check into four factors, each governing a distinct topological property of the PMF:

4. Core Calibration Decisions for K = 20

4.1 Why K = 20 Over K = 10 or K = 100?

Combinatorial sweeps across K ∈ {10, 20, 100} (see summarize_cascade_odds.py) demonstrate that when C = 0.5K and δ = 0.3K are scaled proportionally to K, the cascade probabilities are nearly scale-invariant (differing by < 1.6% between d10 and d20). We chose K = 20 because:

  • It provides twice the granularity of K = 10 for differentiating character attributes (4… 18 vs. 2… 9) and fine-grained environmental/equipment modifiers (± 1, ± 2) without saturating the scale.
  • It avoids the two-digit mental arithmetic bloat of summing 2d100 pairs (2..200), keeping 2d20 sums (2..40) fast and intuitive.

6. Always-Contested Task Resolution & The 1-Pair Tiered GM Resistance Rule

A core pillar of Satya's design philosophy is Epistemic Fog of War: a player knows their own capability (NP, CP), internal state (Δint), and equipment (Δtool), so they can feel how smoothly or clumsily their own execution went—but they should never know for certain whether a high roll was enough to beat an unknown obstacle, or whether a 0-success miss was rescued by an environmental flaw.

6.1 Why Convolving Two Multi-Pair Cascades Destroys Bimodality (and How 1-Pair Tiered GM Resistance Preserves It)

If the GM rolled an independent multi-pair cascade (NGM = 3) against the player's cascade (NP = 5), the net margin Δ M = MP − MGM would be the cross-correlation (convolution of PP(m) with PGM(−m)) of two distributions:

  • Variance Inflation & Skewness Cancellation: Because Var(MP − MGM) = Var(MP) + Var(MGM) and the third central moment subtracts (μ3(MP − MGM) = μ3(MP) − μ3(MGM)), a symmetric multi-pair GM cascade cancels out the player's skewness and pulls the net distribution back toward a Gaussian bell curve.
  • Valley In-Filling in High-Stakes (CumulativeSnowball) Mode: In uncontested CumulativeSnowball (NP = 5, CP = 10), the player's distribution is starkly bimodal (Sarle's Bimodality Coefficient BC = 0.678 > 0.555), with peaks at -1 and +5 separated by a deep watershed valley at +1..+3. If the GM also rolls a bimodal NGM = 3 cascade (peaks at -1 and +3), then Player High (+5) - GM High (+3) = +2 and Player Low (-1) - GM Low (-1) = 0 dump 16.0% of the total probability mass directly into the middle of the valley—collapsing BC from 0.678 (bimodal) down to 0.453 (unimodal)!

By contrast, when the GM rolls a Single 2d20 Pair (1-Pair Tiered GM Resistance) for environmental and passive tasks:

  • The GM's single 2d20 roll is a compact, unimodal triangular distribution with no multi-step carry-over.
  • Convolving the player's NP-pair cascade with a single 2d20 GM pair preserves the bimodal watershed (BC = 0.585 > 0.555) and negative skewness (-0.146) in CumulativeSnowball, while requiring only one hidden 2d20 roll from the GM.

6.2 The Canonical 4-Point Over-Tier & Under-Botch Formula (TieredSinglePair { step_size: 4 })

If a single-pair GM roll only ever generated 0 or 1 success, it would saturate on Challenging (Δext = +4) and Hard (Δext = +8) tasks once Δext is moved off the player's target number—because a competent player (NP = 3 pairs, CP = 10) rolling against their personal TNP = 21 achieves 2+ successes 48.00% of the time, which a binary 0/1 GM roll could never beat!

To give the GM's single 2d20 roll (XGM = AGM + BGM, where AGM, BGM ∈ [1, 20] are the GM's two d20 dice) full multi-success scaling and botch depth using the exact same 4-point (K/5 = 4) tier ruler on both sides of TNGM = 21 − Δext:

  • GM Resistance Successes (SGM — Over-Tiers of 4 Points): When the GM's hidden 2d20 sum XGM = AGM + BGM beats TNGM (XGM > TNGM in strict mode, or ≥ in roll-over), let SGM be the GM's Success Count, BGM be the GM's Botch Count, and MGM be the GM's Signed Margin:
    SGM = 1 + ⌊ XGM − TNGM − 14 ⌋,    BGM = 0,    MGM = +SGM
    • Rolling 1–4 points over TNGM scores 1 GM Success (SGM = 1, MGM = +1).
    • Rolling 5–8 points over TNGM scores 2 GM Successes (SGM = 2, MGM = +2).
    • Rolling 9–12 points over TNGM scores 3 GM Successes (SGM = 3, MGM = +3, and so on, up to 5+ on extreme difficulties).
  • GM Clean 0 or Environmental Botch (BGM — Under-Tiers of 4 Points): When XGM ≤ TNGM:
    SGM = 0,    BGM = min(5,  ⌊ TNGM − XGM4 ⌋),    MGM = −BGM
    • Missing by 0–3 points (TNGM − 3 ≤ XGM ≤ TNGM) is a GM Clean 0 (BGM = 0, MGM = 0).
    • Missing by 4+ points (TNGM − XGM ≥ 4) is a GM Botch (BGM ∈ [1, 5], MGM ∈ [−1, −5]), representing an unexpected weak spot, structural flaw, or momentary lapse in the obstacle!

Even when the player rolls 0 successes (a Clean Near-Miss, MP = 0): They are not guaranteed to fail—there is a 34.00% chance on a Standard task (52.50% on Easy, 19.50% on Challenging) that the GM's hidden roll botches (MGM < 0), rescuing them with a LuckyBreakSuccess!
