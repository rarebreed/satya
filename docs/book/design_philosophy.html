An RPG for a More Civilized Age — Design Philosophy & Goals of Satya

Rust Engine Implementation: crates/satya-engine/src/lib.rs (top-level CARPG simulation crate), crates/satya-engine/src/rules_book.rs (canonical_rules_chapters, search_rules_book), and crates/satya-engine/src/rules_book_html.rs (render_rules_markdown_to_html).

So, what's so special about Satya? Is this some old-school OD&D clone? To explain what Satya is, and the audience this game is catering to, I need to tell a little story first.

1. My Gaming Background

I actually haven't played a role-playing session in close to 30 years now. My gaming career technically started back around 1980 at the ripe old age of 8. My father was an avid historical miniatures gamer, and my older brother had started to play some games including this newfangled D&D. I was more curious about what my father and older brother were doing rather than actually wanting to play myself. But I was curious enough that I asked my parents to buy me the Dungeon Master's Guide, Player's Handbook, Monster Manual, and Deities and Demigods. While a lot of the text went over my head, I was especially enraptured by Deities and Demigods and the Monster Manual. However, I only got to play about 2 or 3 sessions with some of my friends around the 3rd grade. I have no recollection of what we played (I do recall that I was the DM), but it must have been enjoyable.

Fast forward to 1982, and I started playing American Civil War miniatures with my father and his gaming group. I also started playing some Napoleonic-era wargames a little after this time, and also occasionally played some "modern" micro-armor (I don't even think TOW missiles had come out yet as Dragon ATMs were the best infantry could get). Every once in a while, my father's gaming group couldn't make it for some reason, so I wound up having nothing to do on weekends.

However, at the store we played at, the owner had a son who was just a year younger than myself, and we started to hang out. I distinctly remember looking at a tiny pocket box from Steve Jackson called Car Wars, and reading the back thinking it sounded cool. Eventually, a third youngster about my age joined our group, and that's when we started playing RPGs in earnest around late 1983 or early 1984. Ironically, we didn't start with AD&D. Our first role-playing session was either Champions or Top Secret, with many, many more games circa 1984 to 1985. My fondest gaming memories of this time were playing the pulp-era RPG Justice Inc. around 1984, and later Twilight: 2000 also in 1984. 2. The Beginning of the End

I slowly started getting out of RPGs starting around 1988. I still played, but I guess at that point I was 16 and able to drive, and RPGs lost some of their importance to me. I still played once in a blue moon, but not like before. I remember picking up Shadowrun in 1989 and playing a handful of games, but '88 was the beginning of the end. However, probably the most memorable and enjoyable gaming sessions I ever had were in the 1988 time period. And that's because that's when we started playing a campaign set during the Vietnam War using Leading Edge's Phoenix Command rules.

Why was it so memorable to me? I think it was because of its realism. It was the only game I ever ran where the players were actually afraid to get into combat because of how deadly it was. At that age, I was intensely curious about the Vietnam War. When I was about 15, the movie Platoon had come out, and it deeply affected me. I started reading several books on the Vietnam War, including Chickenhawk, We Were Soldiers Once… and Young, and even A Bright Shining Lie. Because the soldiers were reviled (generally speaking) when they got back home, and the horrible hypocrisy of why America was even waging the war, it seemed tailor-made for running some very interesting game sessions.

3. Gaming Isn't Just Escapism

So now, I can finally come to what this game is about. Back in the early days of roleplaying, many of the games featured regular people in unusual circumstances. Look at that list above and here are the games that featured predominantly normal humans without any magical, mystical, or (for the most part) super-technological powers:

  • Justice Inc.
  • James Bond 007
  • Top Secret
  • Aftermath!
  • Bushido
  • Twilight: 2000
  • Traveller (yes, I am including this, because you didn't always have high technology)
  • The Morrow Project (yes, I know they had HAAM suits and fusion reactors)
  • Call of Cthulhu (sure, the monsters were supernatural and had powers, but basically the characters didn't)
  • Phoenix Command
  • Chill (like CoC, generally, the characters didn't have powers)
  • Car Wars
  • Recon (1st & 2nd ed.)

That's probably half the games I listed. While I haven't played an RPG in eons, I have still kept my nose in it, and have been buying RPGs ever since either in dead-tree or electronic formats. What I have noticed in the last 20 years or more is the trend towards Übermenschen with some kind of cool crazy power, whether those powers come from some kind of magical, mystical, or innate nature, or from advanced technology (which, as Mr. Clarke said, can become indistinguishable from magic).

That's not what appeals to me in games, or even in watching movies. I generally love martial arts movies, except when they go into the fantastical wuxia type stuff. My jaw drops when I see Jackie Chan in his peak being able to do what he does in reality. The same with Tony Jaa. I am orders of magnitude more impressed with seeing this level of skill displayed by real humans than watching some crazy FX to simulate powers. I think the last movie or series I enjoyed where the main story involved magical powers was the animated Avatar: The Last Airbender. Series about high tech are a little more palatable to me, at least because it's plausible—plus, the characters often don't have effortless access to their "powers."

And this trend where games cater mostly to characters having some kind of extraordinary power made me wonder: why? Is the market demanding this, or is this what game designers are offering? As I showed in the kinds of games above, close to half the games on the market were about your Everyman (albeit probably highly skilled) facing extraordinary circumstances. When did RPGs have to be all about high tech or fantasy? Perhaps I'm imagining all this, so let's take a look at a snapshot of the top 20 "Hottest Items" on DriveThruRPG (08/14/2020):

  • Star Trek Adventures: Klingon Sourcebook
  • Keyforge: Secrets of the Crucible

Okay, so I'm only going to count the Call of Cthulhu supplements, and even that is a little bit iffy. So I don't think I am imagining that the dominant trend in gaming is fantastical backgrounds and characters with powers. Some might argue that popular games like Tales from the Loop / Things from the Flood should be counted, or maybe the Alien RPG (yeah, it's sci-fi, but the humans don't really have any powers beyond what we could do now), or Free League's Twilight: 2000 4th edition.

Are we really that bored with reality as it is? I often see in forums that people play games to "escape." I get it. Reality sucks, and life is hard. But does that mean we have to run away from reality? I even question the very idea that games should only be about "fun."

4. Exploring Meaning

Have you ever read a good book, watched a good movie, or seen a TV series that you felt made you question things and helped improve your life? Exercise isn't "fun" for many people, at least while they are doing it, but it is something that improves your life. Why do we limit games as just entertainment? Why can't games be provocative and motivating, making us think about things? Roleplaying at its core is not mere entertainment—it's "What if?" I think that a big reason so many suffer nervousness, mental breakdowns, stress, etc., is because we have this irrational and unreal expectation that happiness is what we should all be striving for. And isn't fun a great way to achieve "happiness"?

Perhaps I'm just an oddball. In Buddhism, seeking happiness is not what we should strive for. Rather, we should seek understanding, which in turn begets peace. With happiness, like all things in life, it can be taken away from you. If you are able to find peace, it can't be taken from you. For me, the role-playing sessions I treasured were not like fishing tales boasting of how with only 2 HP left, I was able to slay the dragon or defeat the Lich Lord. They came from small moments, like watching how players would react to a little Vietnamese boy running up to them. Did the little boy have a grenade in his hands? Why is he running towards them? Do I shoot first, ask questions later?

It seems like we are all so desperate to feel special, to feel powerful in this crazy world, that we have forgotten that we are all actually the same. And that's a good thing, not a bad thing. I feel that all this escapism and focusing on all the cool powers is missing a huge aspect of roleplaying. For this game, I want people to question what it would be like if you were in someone's shoes under those circumstances. Some will argue that our characters are an alter-ego and we should not confuse our own self (the player) with the character. While perhaps that is one way to look at it, it is not the only way. I believe the reason many shows, books, and movies do resonate so well is we ask ourselves, "What would I do in his/her place?" If we totally divorce ourselves from our characters, then what is the fundamental point? Is it simply to escape how boring, dull, dreary, or stressful our own lives are? Are we simply being actors, playing a part without relating it to our real self?

So this game's focus will be about settings with little to no super or preternatural beings or powers. If there is magic, it will be background or something that the characters cannot wield easily. For technologically advanced settings, there will be no effortless mind-upload immortality, no magical nanotechnology (nanites still need energy and follow all the laws of thermodynamics), and as with all technology, there will be a price to pay.

What you will see are down-to-earth characters who, like everyone else, have to make decisions with the tools and capabilities they have at hand. If you think that sounds boring, then you are selling yourself short. Heroes are not people with amazing abilities; they are people who make tough choices in tough situations to help people in need. It is, in fact, the difficulty of what the hero faces that makes them and their action amazing, not always how they did it.

5. Goals of Satya: Solving 17 Structural Flaws of Traditional RPGs

*(Note: For a complete reference of all mathematical variables, subscripts, and formulas used below, see the Mathematical Notation, Variables & Formula Glossary).*

Satya has been in the back of my mind for over 25 years. While I don't play as many games as I used to, I am still an avid reader and buyer of games. I still see the same designs over and over which I have become disillusioned with. So it is easiest to describe the 17 structural flaws I dislike in many game systems, and how Satya solves them:

  1. Not varied enough in attributes
  2. Not granular enough in attribute ratings or skill levels
  3. Having artificial cutoffs (e.g., divide by 5 and round up means people buy 13 instead of 15)
  4. Dice systems that are linear in probability (1d20 / d100)
  5. Dice systems that only shift the mode of a static Gaussian bell curve (3d6)
  6. Skill systems that do not differentiate between innate talents and learned physical/knowledge skills
  7. Complete metagame knowledge of probability (no epistemic fog of war in determining chances)
  8. Initiative systems that don't factor in courage or morale
  9. Initiative systems that only consider how soon a character can act, rather than how long the action takes
  10. Lack of personality traits (Psyche) to enforce and/or reward character behavior
  11. Character creation systems that allow choices of skills without lifepath constraints ("How did the professor get Artillery?")
  12. Damage systems that are ablative (HP) rather than inhibiting
  13. Damage systems that don't differentiate between different kinds of anatomical trauma
  14. Systems that do not take metabolic fatigue into consideration
  15. Systems that claim to be generic but require reams of special one-off Feat rules
  16. Combat systems that lack tactical flavor ("a swing is a swing")
  17. Weapons and armor that feel generic because they don't have enough mechanical dimensions that matter

5.1 Lack of Coverage in Attributes

Far too many game systems nowadays have only a handful of attributes, some as few as 3 (e.g., Mind, Body, Spirit). This lack of specialization leads to numerous special "Advantages" to account for differentiation. For example, suppose your game system has Dexterity that implies both hand-eye coordination and overall body gracefulness. If you rank very high in this attribute, does that mean you could be both a world-class microsurgeon and an Olympic gymnast? Probably not, but that's what happens when your system is relegated to just a few attributes. Another disadvantage of having too few attributes is that many characters start to feel the same.

How Satya Solves This

As detailed in Character Characteristics & Aptitudes, Satya separates attributes across five distinct taxonomy groups: Physical (SPD, FOR, DEX, KIN, HLT, FIT, MAS, HGT), Mental (MEM, ANA, INS, CRE, DIS, FOC), Social (COM, PRE, ELO), Sensory (SIG, HEA, TAC, OLF, TAS, ITU), and Psyche (EGO, SLF, OPN, BLG, EXT, CNF, AGG). A character can be lean and explosive (Speed), yet lacking in raw static strength (Force); or have superb recall (Memory), yet struggle to think outside the box (Creativity). This eliminates the need for bolt-on "Advantages" and "Disadvantages" just to describe a realistic human being.

5.2 Lack of Granularity in Ratings and Skills

Closely related to the lack of specialization of attributes is having too narrow a range for attribute or skill ratings. Some systems rate attributes or skills from 0–5. Are people really only rated in 6 buckets? Having too narrow a range means that your probability of success also starts to get wonky, because the jump between an "average" rating of 2 and 5 is either too small to feel meaningful or overwhelmingly large.

How Satya Solves This

In Satya, attributes are rated from 1–20 on the 2d20 Controlling Scale (backed by an underlying 0.0–100.0 continuous spectrum, with human average at C = 10 / 50.0), and skills are rated from 0–10 (1–8 standard human range). This provides the exact resolution needed to distinguish a dabbler (N = 2), a journeyman (N = 3), a seasoned specialist (N = 5), and a grandmaster (N = 8).

5.3 Artificial Cutoffs

Many game systems use artificial step-functions or cutoffs to streamline math. For example, some 2d20 systems cap dice pools at 5, D&D groups ability scores into even-numbered buckets (10–11 = +0, 12–13 = +1), and the Hero System divides attributes by 5 and rounds. The consequence of these cutoffs is a min-maxer's dream: why buy a 15 when a 13 gives the exact same mechanical modifier?

How Satya Solves This

Precision is always preserved in Satya. There are no step-wise modifier buckets: every single point of a Controlling Characteristic (CP ∈ [1, 20]) shifts the player's personal target TNP = 31 − CP + Δtool by 1 on every 2d20 pair, every point of hidden external difficulty (Δext) shifts the GM's resistance target TNGM = 21 − Δext by 1, and every point of Ccombat or skill N adds directly to a character's Tactical Pool (Pbase = Ccombat + 3N).

5.4 Linear and Static-Shape Gaussian Dice Systems

Single-die systems (1d20 or d100) produce flat uniform distributions where every outcome from 1 to 20 is equally likely, making experts feel cartoonishly swingy. Conversely, standard multi-die sum systems (3d6) converge by the Central Limit Theorem to a fixed symmetric Gaussian bell curve; modifiers only slide the mean left or right without changing the shape of the uncertainty. Worse, many dice-pool systems suffer from the "Skilled Botch Paradox," where rolling more dice for higher skill accidentally increases the chance of rolling critical failures.

How Satya Solves This

Satya uses an Always-Contested Two-List Cascading ("Watershed") 2d20 Dice Mechanic (see Probability & Calibration and Core 2d20 Cascade & Task Resolution). Rolling NP sequential pairs of 2d20 where each pair passes positive momentum (+δover = +6) or negative stumble penalty (−δunder = −(6 + Δint)) to the next pair dynamically reshapes the probability distribution's skewness, modality, and tails:

  • Skill (NP) expands the upper tail (+1 to +N_P successes) and gives extra pairs to recover from an early stumble, while strictly reducing botch risk (closest_miss botch evaluation).
  • Controlling Characteristic (CP) shifts the player's personal target TNP = 31 − CP + Δtool across every pair.
  • Internal Fatigue & Shock (Δint) deepens the failure carry penalty (δunder = 6 + Δint) without penalizing Pair 1—meaning an exhausted master who hits their first motion flows through on muscle memory, while a stumble cascades into a heavy penalty.
  • 1-Pair Tiered GM Resistance (TNGM = 21 − Δext, 4-pt Over-Tiers) opposes the player's cascade with a single hidden 2d20 roll by the GM, preserving the player's bimodal watershed topology (BC = 0.585 > 0.555 in high-stakes snowball mode) while keeping task difficulty hidden.

5.5 Innate Talents vs. Learned Physical and Knowledge Skills

Many RPGs treat every skill identically—either letting an untrained character roll at a tiny penalty for brain surgery, or making a character spend skill points just to know how to run, jump, or notice an obvious ambush.

How Satya Solves This

As detailed in Skill System & The 8 Hierarchical Skill Trees, Satya classifies every ability into Talents (TAL) (innate universal capacities like Running, Brawling, Observation, Stealth, Persuasion, and Empathy that every human starts with at N = 1), Physical Skills (PHY) (trained motor/kinesthetic execution starting at N = 0), Knowledge Skills (KNO) (academic/analytical disciplines starting at N = 0), and Hybrid Skills (PHY/KNO) (such as Trauma Surgery or Explosive Ordnance Disposal). Combined with Class A (Open), Class B (Defaultable), and Class C (Hard-Gated Specialist) task rules in Core 2d20 Cascade & Task Resolution, anyone can sprint or throw a punch, but nobody defaults into Neurosurgery or Quantum Cryptography.

5.6 Epistemic Fog of War vs. Metagame Probability

In traditional tabletop games, players know the exact target number and modifiers before they roll ("I need a 14 or higher on 1d20, so I have a 35% chance"), and once the die lands, they immediately know whether they succeeded or failed before the GM says a word—turning tense life-or-death moments into spreadsheet expected-value calculations.

How Satya Solves This

Satya enforces true epistemic fog of war at both the perceptual and mechanical levels: 1. Always-Contested Tasks & The Hidden 1-Pair Tiered GM Resistance Roll: The player rolls their sequential 2d20 cascade against their known personal target number (TNP = 31 − CP + Δtool), while the GM secretly rolls an opposing 2d20 pair against the hidden environmental target (TNGM = 21 − Δext) using 4-point over-success and under-botch tiers (TieredSinglePair { step_size: 4 }). Because Δext is on the GM's hidden roll rather than the player's target number, the player can feel their own execution momentum—yet even when they roll 2 or 3 successes, they never know if it was enough to beat a hardened obstacle (S_P > S_GM), and even when they roll 0 successes (a clean near-miss), a hidden GM botch (MGM < 0) can rescue them with a LuckyBreakSuccess! 2. Sensory-Gated Information: Because Satya is a Computer-Assisted RPG (CARPG), characters do not see raw floating target numbers above an enemy's head. Instead, perceptual skills (Observation, Empathy & Read Person, Clinical Diagnostics, Tactical Recon) reveal qualitative cues according to the character's senses (SIG, HEA, ITU) and expertise (N).

5.7 Courage, OODA Loops, and the Count-Up Action Clock

Most turn-based initiative systems only ask "Who acts first?" and then put everyone on a rigid cyclic carousel where a heavy two-handed haymaker takes the exact same "1 Action" as a lightning jab. Furthermore, traditional initiative ignores whether a combatant actually has the nerve to step out of cover when bullets are snapping past their head.

How Satya Solves This

Satya replaces cyclic rounds with a continuous Count-Up Action Clock anchored by the character's 4-Pillar OODA Loop (⌊(ITU + INS + Dcog + SPD) / 4⌋), Cognitive Decision Speed (Dcog = ⌊(max(ANA, CRE) + FOC) / 2⌋), and Discipline (DIS) Morale state (see Action Economy, OODA & Multi-Sensory Perception and Tactical Triad & Combat Pools):

  • Every maneuver has a Tick Cost (TC = TC_decide + TC_act) that determines when the action finishes, modified by how much Tempo the fighter invests and degraded by action_time_factor from Fatigue and Wound Shock.
  • A fast probe (High Tempo) can interrupt a slow, telegraphed haymaker (Low Tempo) mid-swing.
  • Combatants under fire must pass Discipline (DIS) Composure & Morale Checks or drop through five morale conditions (High, Steady, Wavering, Broken, Panic), directly constraining how they can allocate their Tactical Pool.

5.8 Quantified Psyche, Behavioral Friction, and Social Combat

In most games, personality alignments or traits are either toothless flavor text that players ignore whenever convenient, or rigid straitjackets. Likewise, social encounters are often reduced to a single binary Persuasion roll.

How Satya Solves This

Satya defines seven Psyche Characteristics (Ego, Selfishness, Openness, Belongingness, Extraversion, Conformity, and Aggressiveness on the 1–20 scale; see Psyche, Morale & Social Combat):

  • Psyche Friction & Drift: Acting violently against a core Psyche trait triggers a DIS Friction Check (TN = 31 − DIS + |P − 10|), while repeated choices organically shift personality over time via Psyche Drift. Conversely, risking everything for a core conviction triggers a Conviction Surge.
  • Social Combat & Cognitive Dissonance: Arguments resonate with or clash against specific Psyche traits. When a speaker crafts an argument that simultaneously resonates with one strong trait (e.g., hard scientific proof appealing to high Openness) while clashing with another (e.g., tribal loyalty in high Belongingness), it inflicts Cognitive Dissonance ("Mental Bleed")—forcing the target to convert, harden into dogmatic denial, compartmentalize, or snap.

5.9 Lifepath Grounding and Hierarchical Skill Prerequisites

Point-buy character creation systems routinely allow absurd min-maxed combinations—such as an ivory-tower professor who inexplicably has rank-5 Heavy Machine Guns or a street teen who knows Aerospace Engineering without knowing Algebra.

How Satya Solves This

In Lifepath Character Creation, Economy & Heat, characters develop through chronological Lifepath Stages (Socioeconomic Origin, Elementary, Junior High, High School vs. Street Dropout, and Adult Career Terms). Academic progression uses study rolls governed by Discipline (DIS) + Mental Characteristic + Domain Aptitude (APT) modified by School Quality (SQM), while advanced skills require both Skill Tree Prerequisites (e.g., Arithmetic → Algebra → Trigonometry → Calculus → Engineering) and Institutional Access Tags ([Access: Academic], [Access: Restricted], [Access: Illicit]).

5.10 Anatomical Trauma, Wound Shock, and Fatigue Over Hit Points

Ablative Hit Points treat a human body like a progress bar: a fighter with 80 HP fights at 100% peak efficiency at 1 HP until the final point is depleted, and healing rules absurdly take longer to restore "luck and grit."

How Satya Solves This

Satya eliminates Hit Points in favor of Anatomical Wound Tracks, Systemic Shock, and Fatigue (see Health, Anatomical Trauma, Fatigue & Mental Conditions):

  • Fatigue (fatigue_level) and Wound Shock (wound_shock_index) drain -2 points per tier from the combatant's Effective Tactical Pool (Peff), increase internal cascade difficulty (Δint, deepening −δunder), and slow every action via action_time_factor.
  • Localized Structural Wounds directly slash the corresponding Aspect Ceiling (Tmax for leg wounds, Cmax for arm/head/eye wounds, Fmax for shoulder/torso wounds)—so a sliced calf immediately hobbles a fighter's Tempo, and a fractured wrist ruins their Control.

5.11 The Tactical Triad (Tempo, Control, Force) Over Feat Bloat

Many systems try to add tactical depth to combat by publishing hundreds of special-case "Feats," "Maneuvers," or "Cards" (Power Attack, Combat Expertise, Riposte, Double Tap), forcing players to memorize exceptions while weapons still feel like reskinned damage dice.

How Satya Solves This

Satya's "Envelope & Fill" Tactical Pool Architecture (see Tactical Triad & Combat Pools, Melee Combat, and Ranged Combat, Suppression & Armor) derives every maneuver from a single unified mechanic:

  • Your Characteristics (SPD, DEX/KIN/SIG, FOR) set your physical Aspect Ceilings (Tmax, Cmax, Fmax—the Envelope), while your Combat Readiness and Skill (N) determine your Tactical Pool (Pbase = Ccombat + 3N—the Fill).
  • On every exchange, splitting your pool across Tempo (T — speed & mobility), Control (C — precision & guard), and Force (F — impact power, recoil bracing & rooted balance) naturally produces jabs/probes (TC), blitzes/dives (TF), planted finishers/braced fire (CF), and counter-initiative traps—without a single bolt-on Feat rule.
