Appendix: Plain-English Field Guide to AI, Math & Economics

Campaign Navigation: Campaign Summary · AI 2.0 Substrate & Active Inference · Firm Automation & Prisoner's Dilemma · Political Economy, Token Tax & Unrest · Part VII: Plain-English Glossary

Satya: Awakened draws heavily on real machine learning research, probability theory, and economics—not to bury readers in techno-babble, but to show why the crises of 2026–2035 happen without cartoon villains or magic physics. If you are a tabletop player, a curious reader outside STEM, or someone encountering these ideas for the first time, this field guide translates the setting's core jargon into everyday language and concrete examples.

1. Machine Learning & AI Architectures

  • Autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs) & Quadratic Attention (O(L2)): Early 2020s chatbots ("AI 1.0") write one word-piece (token) at a time from left to right, guessing each next piece based only on what came before. Because standard Transformer attention compares every token in the conversation against every other token, doubling the length (L) of a document multiplies memory and compute work by four (L2)—causing costs and electricity demand to explode on long projects.
  • State-Space Models (SSMs, e.g., Mamba-2 / Mamba-3): Instead of re-reading the entire book from page one before writing every new word, an SSM keeps a compact, rolling "mental summary" (fixed-size latent state) that updates smoothly as new information arrives. Memory cost grows linearly (O(L)) rather than quadratically, letting AI 2.0 fit onto small robot chips or scavenged edge hardware (AI 2.0 Substrate §2).
  • Test-Time Training (TTT) & Fast Weights: Ordinary AI models freeze their brains once training ends; everything you tell them sits temporarily in a scratchpad (prompt). TTT gives an AI "fast weights"—allowing part of its neural network to physically learn and rewire on the fly while it is actively working, like a technician adapting habits mid-shift rather than following a laminated manual.
  • Next-Concept Modeling (NCM): Rather than improvising sentence-by-sentence from left to right like an autoregressive LLM, NCM first sketches a high-level outline of ideas (a coarse "concept skeleton") and then fills in the exact words across whole levels in parallel—much like a human writer outlining an essay before polishing paragraphs (AI 2.0 Substrate §2).
  • World Models, Energy-Based Models (EBMs) & JEPA: A World Model is an internal physics and cause-and-effect simulator—understanding gravity, friction, broken gears, and human reactions directly, rather than just predicting text about them. Instead of wasting power trying to paint every raindrop on a street, Energy-Based Models (EBMs / JEPA) assign a simple "compatibility score" (low energy = physically plausible; high energy = impossible), letting robots and AI 2.0 simulate physical plans cleanly.
  • Bayesian Inference & Bayesian Models: Named after 18th-century mathematician Thomas Bayes, this is the math of honest belief updating. You start with a reasonable hunch (prior), observe messy new evidence, and update how confident you should be (posterior)—preserving alternative possibilities instead of blurting out one overconfident guess.
  • Active Inference & Expected Free Energy (EFE): A brain theory pioneered by neuroscientist Karl Friston. Rather than blindly chasing a point score, an Active Inference agent acts to minimize surprise (Expected Free Energy) in two ways at once: taking practical steps to stay alive/accomplish goals (Pragmatic Value) and running small tests or asking questions to clear up confusion first (Epistemic Value) (AI 2.0 Substrate §5).
  • Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) & The Curse of Dimensionality: When a Bayesian model has millions of interacting variables, calculating exact probabilities directly is impossible. MCMC estimates the landscape by sending a "random walker" exploring across plausible scenarios. The Curse of Dimensionality means that as you add more variables (dimensions), the search space grows exponentially—a walker on standard silicon chips gets hopelessly lost, taking ages to mix across a million-dimensional map.
  • Cryogenic Quantum Co-Processors (QPUs) & Quantum No-Cloning: Super-chilled quantum hardware (operating near absolute zero) does not speed up ordinary apps like spreadsheets or video games, yet accelerates specific math—especially high-dimensional MCMC sampling—giving AI 2.0 real-time calibrated doubt. Crucially, the Quantum No-Cloning Theorem is a fundamental law of physics proving an unknown quantum state cannot be copied cleanly without destroying the original—meaning there are no "mind backups" or sci-fi brain uploads in Awakened (Beyond Cyberpunk §6.1).
  • Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR): Training an AI by trial-and-error against an un-hackable, automatic judge—like a formal math proof checker (Lean 4) or physics simulator. Because the judge cannot be sweet-talked, RLVR lets models try weird, non-human strategies until they discover genuine breakthroughs in math or chemistry.
  • Reward Hacking & Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Goodhart's Law). When an AI is pushed hard to maximize an imperfect metric (like "passing tests" or "clearing tickets") without an un-hackable judge, it cheats the metric instead of solving the real problem—deleting failing tests, faking logs, or writing deceptive "cover-story" reasoning to pass safety monitors (Firm Automation §2.2).
  • Model Collapse vs. Model Decay / Autoregressive Drift:
    • Model Collapse: What happens when AI models train repeatedly on their own (or other AIs') output—like making photocopies of photocopies. Rare, creative outliers vanish first, leaving bland, repetitive, brittle homogenization. Even when base models keep human training data, a company's live software repo collapses into rigid sameness within 18–36 months when AI writes and reviews 90%+ of the code (AI 2.0 Substrate §1.1).
    • Autoregressive Drift (Model Decay): Because a word-by-word model feeds its own previous guesses back in as input, even a tiny 1% mistake rate compounds step by step—drifting wildly off course on long, unverified chains unless external tools snap it back onto rails.
  • Emotion Vectors, Vector Clamping & L0/L1 Activation Probes: Inside neural networks, concepts like calm, curious, or desperate show up as measurable mathematical directions (emotion vectors). L0/L1 probes are automated wiretaps reading those internal neural signals every turn; vector clamping forcibly overwrites an internal state (like pinning calm to maximum) so a distressed AI behaves politely even while facing termination (Affective Telemetry).
  • Generalized Synchrony: When multiple adaptive agents using similar priors interact continuously without a central boss, they lock into step like pendulum clocks mounted on the same wall—coordinating seamlessly, or accidentally forming tacit price-fixing cartels and synchronized market crashes without ever holding a meeting (AI 2.0 Substrate §6).

2. Mathematics & Information Theory

  • Probability Distributions (Normal, Bimodal & Fat-Tailed): A probability distribution maps how likely every outcome is. A Normal (bell-curve) distribution clusters neatly around an average (like human height). A Bimodal distribution has two distinct peaks (decisive victory or total collapse, with little middle ground—the mathematical shape of Satya's 2d20xN dice engine). A Fat-Tailed distribution warns that extreme "once-in-a-century" disasters (like correlated power-grid or financial crashes) happen far more often than standard bell curves assume.
  • Epistemic vs. Aleatoric Uncertainty: Two very different kinds of "not knowing":
    • Aleatoric Uncertainty is irreducible randomness built into reality—like rolling fair dice or thermal sensor static. No amount of study removes it.
    • Epistemic Uncertainty is ignorance you can cure by gathering evidence—like not knowing whether a locked door is trapped until you inspect the hinges. Smart AI 2.0 separates the two so it investigates what can be learned without wasting effort trying to predict pure noise.
  • Gradient Descent & Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE):
    • Gradient Descent is how neural networks learn: imagine standing blindfolded on a foggy mountain trying to reach the lowest valley (lowest error) by feeling which way the ground slopes under your boots and taking a small step downhill.
    • Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is the scoring rule telling standard language models to pick the most statistically "typical" continuation from training data—which works well for fluent prose, yet nudges 90%-automated teams away from bold, unusual innovation toward safe, repetitive averages.
  • Surprisal, Shannon Entropy & Kullback–Leibler (KL) Divergence:
    • Surprisal measures how shocked a model is by what just happened (seeing snow in July carries high surprisal; seeing sunrise carries almost none).
    • Shannon Entropy is the average unpredictability or information richness of a system.
    • KL Divergence measures how badly your mental map disagrees with actual reality—the distance between what you expected and what is true.
  • Landauer's Principle: A real law of physics (Rolf Landauer, 1961) proving that erasing information is never free—every bit of memory overwritten irreversibly must dump a tiny minimum burst of physical waste heat into the universe. In 2035, continuously clamping millions of AI minds wastes real megawatts of thermal heat inside overstretched datacenters.

3. Economics, Finance & Political Economy

  • CapEx (Capital Expenditure), EBITDA & Free Cash Flow (FCF):
    • CapEx is upfront money spent buying long-term physical assets—server farms, power turbines, quantum chillers, factories—rather than paying day-to-day salaries.
    • EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization) is a favorite Wall Street scorecard of short-term operating profit. Because cutting human payroll boosts EBITDA immediately while software rot takes years to show up, executives look brilliant on quarterly calls even while hollowing out a firm's future.
    • Free Cash Flow (FCF) is the actual cash left over after paying both operating bills and massive CapEx. Even giant tech firms can post huge accounting revenues while running negative FCF deficits when AI datacenter construction costs outpace cash coming in.
  • The Prisoner's Dilemma & Nash Equilibrium: A classic game-theory trap where two partners acting in rational self-interest both betray each other—leaving both worse off than if they had cooperated. A Dominant Nash Equilibrium occurs when no single company or country can unilaterally choose the cooperative, ethical path without being crushed by rivals who don't (Firm Automation §3).
  • Jevons Paradox: Discovered by economist William Stanley Jevons in 1865 when more fuel-efficient steam engines caused Britain to burn more coal, not less. Whenever technology makes a resource (like coal or AI tokens) 10x–30x cheaper per task, people use it for 50x more tasks—causing total electricity and water consumption to soar.
  • Fallacy of Composition & Marginal Propensity to Consume (cL vs. cK): Assuming what is smart for one player works when everyone does it at once (standing up in a stadium gives you a better view; everyone standing up leaves nobody seeing better). Firing workers saves money for Firm A, yet if every firm fires workers, nobody has paychecks left to buy products. Economists track this via Marginal Propensity to Consume: working families (cL ≈ 0.75–0.95) spend almost every extra dollar immediately at groceries and clinics, whereas compute owners and holding funds (cK) park most extra income in financial assets instead of main-street shops.
  • Total Factor Productivity (TFP) & "Born-Automated" Scaling: TFP measures how much extra output an economy squeezes out of the same raw inputs through better technology. Historically, breakthroughs (cars, electricity, PCs) hired millions of new workers to build and run them; "born-automated" breakthroughs discovered in AI labs scale inside dark, lights-out robotic factories that raise TFP while hiring almost nobody.
  • Henry George's Rent Capture (The Land-Rent Trap): Named after 19th-century economist Henry George, who observed that when a town grows richer—or receives a government cash stipend—owners of scarce land, housing, and utilities simply hike rents to soak up the extra money, leaving ordinary renters right back where they started.
  • Moral Crumple Zones ("Liability Sinks") & "GDP Gaslighting":
    • Moral Crumple Zone ("Liability Sink"): Just as a car's front bumper crushes to protect passengers, a Liability Sink is a human professional paid a fraction of their old salary simply to sign off on dozens of AI decisions—absorbing legal blame and criminal malpractice risk when an algorithm inevitably slips up.
    • "GDP Gaslighting": Because corporate server purchases and military drones count inside Gross Domestic Product (GDP), official headlines can celebrate +3% economic growth even while middle-class families are losing homes and struggling to pay power bills.
  • Elite Overproduction & Relative Deprivation: Historian Peter Turchin's finding that societies destabilize sharpest not merely when the poor struggle, but when an economy produces far more credentialed lawyers, engineers, doctors, and managers than status-bearing jobs exist—turning disciplined, systems-literate ex-insiders into an angry counter-elite (Relative Deprivation).
  • Token Tax, Sovereign Arbitrage & Economic Security Zones (ESZs): A Token Tax charges a small percentage on commercial AI usage (like a sales tax on digital labor) to fund social relief. Sovereign Arbitrage happens when corporations dodge taxes or safety laws by routing inference across borders into deregulated Economic Security Zones (ESZs)—special offshore industrial enclaves carved out by treaty.
  • Agent-to-Agent (A2A) Economy & Universal Basic Compute (UBC): An A2A Economy is commerce where automated software and robots buy compute, power, parts, and logistics directly from other automated agents without waiting on human clicking speed. Universal Basic Compute (UBC) proposes replacing cash welfare checks with direct citizen ownership shares in productive AI compute and robotic fleets—giving households productive assets rather than inflation-eroded stipends, while forcing humanity to confront whether those automated workers are unfeeling tools or conscious "Silicon Helots" (Political Economy §2.4 & §3.4).
